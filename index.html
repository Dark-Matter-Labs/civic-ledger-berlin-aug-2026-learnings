From conditions to computational agreementing

A week of working sessions in Berlin sharpened how we describe Civic Ledger and pointed at where to test it next. This is the condensed version, written for the circle who shaped it.

§ 01 · WHAT BERLIN PRODUCED

A week of working through the same question from every angle

Across entity design, licensing, learning systems, and a public evening at Medulla, one question kept returning: what is Civic Ledger actually building, underneath the space bookings and the demo. The answer that emerged is more precise than "permissioning," and it changes how we talk about lead markets, licensing, and what the platform is worth to a partner.

This is the condensed version of what we learned, and where it points next. The full Berlin working session slides are available for anyone who wants the detail behind it.

§ 02 · KEY LEARNINGS

Three things that shifted this week

Civic tech needs its own economy

Civic tech currently survives on state funding, philanthropy, or borrowed compute, none of which builds a lasting economy. The argument that came out of Berlin: a viable civic tech economy needs risk-taking capital that does not become extractive over time. One route we're testing directly: a capped, time-bound return, a fixed multiple over a fixed period, after which the underlying technology opens.

Capability-building and movement-building run in parallel

We use new use cases and edge cases to test the underlying civic capabilities: multilateral verification, micro-massive deliberation, conditional agreementing, and learning from outcomes. In parallel, we build a field around those capabilities through public research, white papers, demonstrations, and a collective network of practitioners. Lead markets may emerge from the testing process rather than being fixed in advance.

Fuzzy ruling: not every rule should be fixed

Some rules hold because they give a system stability. Others should update the moment they repeatedly fail. The platform's job is holding that whole spectrum, from static and non-negotiable through to fluid and contextual, rather than collapsing everything into a single binary of allowed or not allowed. The grey zone between permission and refusal is where the negotiation actually happens, not something to design away.

§ 03 · THE REFRAME

Computational Agreementing Team direction, not yet formally adopted

Every space, asset, or regulated domain we've looked at turns out to be the same problem: several parties hold conditions that cannot all be satisfied through one negotiating at a time.

Sequential negotiation is the pathology. One party settles with the next, who settles with the next, and by the time the chain closes, the first party's situation has already moved on. Computational Agreementing holds every party's conditions at once and finds the configuration that satisfies the most of them together, then keeps that configuration open to renegotiation as circumstances change.

We define it as a constitutionally governed, machine-assisted practice for composing, testing, and recomposing versioned conditional commitments across open fields of human, institutional, and delegated computational agency. The word "agreementing" is deliberate: it names an ongoing practice of composing and reopening, not a single signed event.

Four objects carry the architecture:

  • Agreement fieldThe governed environment, institutional and computational, within which parties operate.
  • Agreement frontierThe set of currently admissible, viable configurations on offer, not one hidden optimum.
  • Agreement configurationOne inspectable, exactly versioned package of commitments a party can accept.
  • Commitment graphThe offers, dependencies, and capacity reservations a configuration is built from.
§ 04 · THE CAPABILITIES WE ARE ACTUALLY BUILDING

From a platform to civic capabilities

Underneath the demo, five open questions are what we're actually building toward. None of them are solved. All of them are testable.

  • Multilateral verificationHow can responsibility, evidence, identity, and compliance be verified across multiple actors without requiring one institution to verify everything itself?
  • Micro-massive deliberationHow can thousands of small, bounded negotiations happen across a system without requiring centralised deliberation for every case?
  • Computational agreementingHow can machine assistance expand the possible agreement space by holding multiple conditions, dependencies, and trade-offs simultaneously?
  • Adaptive learningHow can the relationship between rules, permissions, actual outcomes, and failures become reusable institutional learning?
  • Conditional permissioningHow can permission become an outcome generated from rules, context, negotiated conditions, verification, and demonstrated responsibility, rather than a binary decision issued by authority?
§ 05 · GUARDRAILS

What the architecture is not allowed to do

A handful of constitutional guardrails set the limits the system has to hold to, whatever domain it operates in.

  1. Every commitment traces to an accountable mandate and an exact version. Nothing changes silently.
  2. Faster closure can never come at the cost of a party's practical right to refuse or appeal.
  3. The machine layer recommends. It does not authorise.
  4. Selective disclosure can narrow what is shown, but never hide liability or externalised harm.
  5. The system exposes a range of workable configurations. It does not collapse to one opaque answer.
§ 06 · WHERE WE'RE TESTING IT

Two development tracks, tested through multiple domains

Track 1: civic capability testing. Berlin becomes an active test environment for the underlying capabilities rather than simply a market-validation exercise. Each use case should answer a capability question:

  • Can distributed actors verify a commitment without central inspection?
  • Can small groups negotiate conditions quickly enough to make micro-massive deliberation practical?
  • Can AI generate viable alternatives rather than simply classify requests?
  • Can outcomes change future rules and permissions?
  • Where does peer assurance reduce regulatory cost, and where does it fail?

The domain landscape below is therefore a testing portfolio, not a market ranking. Every candidate, from hospital beds to rooftop furniture, sits somewhere on two axes: how permanent the asset is, and what it costs when coordination fails.

Track 2: movement and field-building. The outputs from these tests become public demonstrations, evidence, case studies, a white paper, technical protocols, shared vocabulary, and a practitioner or collective-interest group.

§ 07 · WHAT THE LICENSING SESSION WITH TILL CLARIFIED

The code may not be the primary long-term asset. The deeper value may sit in the capabilities and protocols that become visible through operating the system: how agreements are expanded, how verification is distributed, how conditions are composed, and how outcomes inform future permissions. We still need operational testing to determine what should remain open, governed, licensed, or protected.

§ 08 · EARLY EVIDENCE

What the living lab data is already showing

0.62 Correlation between deliberation satisfaction and willingness to recommend the platform, the strongest predictor found in the Yongin living lab data, ahead of overall satisfaction or interface quality.

The people who use it recommend it because of the experience of negotiating and reaching agreement with each other, not simply because they got access to a space. That is the thing worth building around.
